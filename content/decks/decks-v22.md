## mg-b-01 · Hiring and interviewing
*Skills:* hiring, interviewing · *Check yourself:* mg-b-01-q1

**1. What it is — Building the team on purpose**
- Hiring decides who joins, which shapes everything the team can do for years.
- A good process is fair, consistent and predictive of the job.

**2. Core ideas — Define, structure, decide**
- Write down what the role needs before interviewing: skills, level, what success looks like at six months.
- Structured interviews: each interviewer covers set areas with set questions and a scoring guide.
- Work-like exercises predict better than puzzles.
- Decide on evidence against the criteria, not on 'gut feel' or likeability.

**3. The trade-offs — Speed against rigour**
- Slow processes lose good candidates; rushed ones hire mistakes.
- Hiring for 'culture fit' can quietly mean hiring people like you; hire for values and what the team lacks.
- Too many rounds exhaust candidates without improving decisions.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- Interview training and calibration sessions.
- Debriefs where each interviewer writes feedback before discussion.
- A candidate experience survey.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-b-01-q1).** In a debrief, the first interviewer says 'strong hire', and everyone agrees. Some had doubts. What process prevents this?
- ✅ Each interviewer submits written feedback against the criteria before the debrief, then discusses evidence — *Independent written feedback avoids anchoring and groupthink.*
- Let the most senior person decide — *One voice isn't evidence.*
- Hire everyone who's liked — *Likeability isn't the criteria.*
- Skip debriefs — *Removes the check.*

## mg-b-02 · Planning and estimation
*Skills:* planning, estimation · *Check yourself:* mg-b-02-q1

**1. What it is — Saying what we'll do, and roughly when**
- Planning chooses what the team works on; estimation gives a sense of size and time so others can plan too.
- Estimates are forecasts with uncertainty, not promises.

**2. Core ideas — Slice, size, forecast**
- Break work into small, shippable slices; small things are easier to estimate and deliver.
- Size relatively (this is bigger than that) rather than in exact hours.
- Use past throughput to forecast: how many slices the team actually finishes per week.
- Give ranges and confidence ('likely 4–6 weeks'), and update as you learn.

**3. The trade-offs — Certainty is expensive**
- Detailed up-front plans go stale; rolling plans adapt.
- Padding hides risk; naming risks is better.
- Committing to scope, date and quality all at once rarely works; choose which flexes.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- Quarterly goals with monthly check-ins.
- Risks listed next to the plan.
- Scope cut, not quality, when dates are fixed.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-b-02-q1).** A stakeholder asks for an exact delivery date for a large, unclear project. What's the most honest response?
- ✅ Give a range based on what's known, name the biggest unknowns, and commit to updating it as the first slices ship — *Ranges with risks are honest and useful; updates keep trust.*
- Promise a date to keep them happy — *Sets up a broken promise.*
- Refuse to estimate — *Unhelpful.*
- Double your guess and say it's exact — *Still false precision.*

## mg-b-03 · Stakeholder management
*Skills:* stakeholder management, communication · *Check yourself:* mg-b-03-q1

**1. What it is — Keeping the people around the team aligned**
- Stakeholders are the people affected by or able to affect your team's work: product, other teams, leaders, customers.
- Managing them is mostly communication and expectations.

**2. Core ideas — Know them, inform them, involve them**
- Map who cares about what, and how much influence they have.
- Share progress and risks regularly, in their terms, before they ask.
- Bring bad news early, with options.
- Agree decisions and who makes them, in writing.

**3. The trade-offs — Everyone can't get everything**
- Saying yes to everyone overloads the team; say no with reasons and alternatives.
- Too many updates are noise; too few breed surprises.
- Escalating is sometimes right; doing it without warning burns trust.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- A short regular update: done, next, risks, asks.
- Decision logs.
- Roadmap conversations that start from goals, not feature lists.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-b-03-q1).** Your team will miss a date another team depends on. When should you tell them?
- ✅ As soon as you know, with the reason, the new estimate and options to reduce their impact — *Early bad news lets them adapt; late news costs them.*
- On the due date — *Too late to adjust.*
- Only if they ask — *They're relying on you.*
- After you've finished — *Far too late.*

## mg-p-01 · Delivery and execution
*Skills:* execution, delivery · *Check yourself:* mg-p-01-q1

**1. What it is — Getting the right things finished**
- Execution is turning plans into shipped results, repeatedly, without burning out the team.
- Most delivery problems are too much work in progress and unclear priorities.

**2. Core ideas — Focus, flow, feedback**
- Limit work in progress: finishing beats starting.
- One clear priority order the whole team knows.
- Remove blockers daily; that's much of a manager's job.
- Measure flow (cycle time, throughput), not hours or busyness.

**3. The trade-offs — Busy isn't productive**
- Starting many things at once makes everything late.
- Process added to fix one slip often slows everything.
- Heroics hide problems and burn people out.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- A visible board with WIP limits.
- Short check-ins focused on blockers.
- Retrospectives that produce one or two real changes.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-p-01-q1).** A team has 14 projects in progress for 6 engineers, and everything is late. What's the first lever?
- ✅ Cut work in progress: finish the top few before starting more — *Fewer things at once means each finishes sooner.*
- Ask everyone to work weekends — *Burns people out, doesn't fix flow.*
- Add a daily status report — *Reporting doesn't create time.*
- Start more projects to show progress — *Makes it worse.*

## mg-p-02 · Performance management
*Skills:* performance management, growth · *Check yourself:* mg-p-02-q1

**1. What it is — Helping people do their best work**
- Performance management sets expectations, gives feedback, supports growth, and deals with problems fairly.
- Nothing in a review should be a surprise.

**2. Core ideas — Expectations, evidence, support**
- Clear expectations for each level (a career ladder) and each person's goals.
- Regular feedback with examples, so reviews summarise rather than reveal.
- Growth plans with real opportunities, not just advice.
- Underperformance addressed early, with a specific plan, support and timeline.

**3. The trade-offs — Kind isn't avoiding**
- Delaying hard conversations is unkind to the person and the team.
- Ratings invite gaming; focus on impact and behaviour.
- Bias creeps in; calibrate across managers and use evidence.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- Calibration sessions across teams.
- Written growth plans reviewed in one-to-ones.
- Documented performance plans when needed.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-p-02-q1).** An engineer gets a 'below expectations' rating with no earlier feedback about it. What went wrong?
- ✅ Feedback wasn't given along the way; reviews should summarise known feedback, not surprise — *Surprises in reviews mean feedback failed earlier.*
- The rating scale — *The scale isn't the issue.*
- The engineer should have guessed — *Expectations must be stated.*
- Nothing — *It damages trust.*

## mg-p-03 · Team health
*Skills:* team health, psychological safety · *Check yourself:* mg-p-03-q1

**1. What it is — A team that works well together, sustainably**
- A healthy team shares information, disagrees openly, learns from mistakes and doesn't burn out.
- Psychological safety, the belief that you can speak up without being punished, underlies it.

**2. Core ideas — Safety, clarity, sustainability**
- Make it safe to say 'I don't know', 'I made a mistake' and 'I disagree'.
- Clear roles and goals reduce friction.
- Watch for burnout signs: long hours, cynicism, withdrawal.
- Run retrospectives and act on them.

**3. The trade-offs — Comfort isn't the goal**
- Safe doesn't mean conflict-free; it means conflict about ideas is fine.
- Surveys measure some things and miss others; talk to people.
- Protecting the team sometimes means saying no upward.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- Blameless postmortems.
- A sustainable on-call rotation.
- Managers who admit their own mistakes first.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-p-03-q1).** After a manager criticised an engineer publicly for an outage, people stopped reporting small mistakes. What's the fix?
- ✅ Rebuild safety: own the mistake publicly, move to blameless reviews, and thank people for raising problems — *Speaking up returns when it's safe and valued.*
- Punish anyone who hides mistakes — *Fear deepens hiding.*
- Add more monitoring only — *Helps, but the culture problem remains.*
- Ignore it — *Problems will grow unseen.*

## mg-p-04 · Leading through incidents
*Skills:* incident leadership, management · *Check yourself:* mg-p-04-q1

**1. What it is — The manager's role when things break**
- During a serious incident, managers protect the responders, coordinate, and communicate upward and outward.
- Afterwards, they make sure the lessons turn into change.

**2. Core ideas — Shield, support, follow up**
- Don't take over the technical response; support the incident commander.
- Keep executives and customers informed so engineers can focus.
- Watch for fatigue; rotate people on long incidents.
- Make sure postmortem actions get time and owners.

**3. The trade-offs — Presence without pressure**
- Hovering over responders slows them down.
- Pushing for a cause before the fire is out costs time.
- Promising 'never again' without investment rings hollow.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- A communications lead role separate from the technical lead.
- Food, breaks and handovers on long incidents.
- Postmortem actions tracked in planning.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-p-04-q1).** During a major outage, a director keeps joining the response channel asking engineers for updates every few minutes. What should the manager do?
- ✅ Set up a separate channel with regular updates from a comms lead, so responders can focus — *Separating communication from response protects focus and keeps leaders informed.*
- Ask engineers to answer faster — *Adds pressure, slows the fix.*
- Remove the director — *Not necessary with a better channel.*
- Pause the response to brief them — *Delays recovery.*

## mg-s-01 · Technical strategy
*Skills:* technical strategy, architecture · *Check yourself:* mg-s-01-q1

**1. What it is — Choosing technical direction for the long term**
- Technical strategy decides where the systems should go over the next years and why: platforms, architecture, build or buy.
- Good strategy is a few clear choices, explained.

**2. Core ideas — Diagnosis, policy, actions**
- Diagnose the real problem (slow delivery, outages, cost), with evidence.
- Set guiding choices ('one way to run services', 'buy, don't build, for non-core needs').
- Plan concrete steps, with milestones and owners.
- Write it down and share it, so teams make consistent decisions.

**3. The trade-offs — Choices mean saying no**
- A strategy that allows everything guides nothing.
- Big rewrites rarely pay off; incremental migration usually wins.
- Strategy without investment is a wish.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- Architecture decision records.
- Tech radar or standards documents.
- Strategy reviewed yearly against what happened.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-s-01-q1).** Teams use five different ways to deploy services, causing outages and slow onboarding. What's a strategic response?
- ✅ Choose one supported way, explain why, and plan an incremental migration with support for teams — *A clear choice with a migration path reduces cost and risk.*
- Rewrite all services at once — *Big-bang rewrites are risky.*
- Let each team keep choosing — *That's the cause.*
- Ban deployments — *Not workable.*

## mg-s-02 · Growing managers
*Skills:* management development, leadership · *Check yourself:* mg-s-02-q1

**1. What it is — Developing the next leaders**
- As organisations grow, senior managers spend more time growing managers than managing engineers directly.
- New managers need support; the job changes more than most expect.

**2. Core ideas — Transition, coaching, practice**
- The switch from engineer to manager changes how success is measured: through the team, not personal output.
- Coach rather than solve: ask questions that help them find their answer.
- Give them real responsibility with a safety net.
- Peer groups and training help new managers learn from each other.

**3. The trade-offs — Not everyone should manage**
- Promoting the best engineer into management can lose a great engineer and gain an unhappy manager.
- A parallel senior engineering track keeps strong engineers growing.
- Too much hand-holding slows their growth; too little sets them up to fail.

**4. In practice — What you'll see at work**
- Manager onboarding plans.
- Regular skip-level conversations.
- Trial periods for engineers trying management.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-s-02-q1).** A new manager keeps doing the hardest technical work themselves and their team is stalling. How do you coach them?
- ✅ Help them see their impact now comes through the team: delegate the hard work with support, and spend their time unblocking and growing people — *The core transition is from doing to enabling.*
- Tell them to code more — *Deepens the problem.*
- Take over their team — *Removes their chance to learn.*
- Ignore it — *The team keeps stalling.*

## mg-iv-01 · People scenarios
*Skills:* management interview, people · *Check yourself:* mg-iv-01-q1

**1. What it is — 'What would you do if…?' about people**
- Manager interviews ask about conflicts, low performance, a resignation, a burnt-out engineer.
- They're scoring empathy, directness and judgement.

**2. Core ideas — Listen, clarify, act**
- Start by understanding: what's really happening, from each side.
- Be direct about expectations and kind in how you say them.
- Act with a plan and follow up.
- Use real examples from your own experience.

**3. The trade-offs — Neither soft nor harsh**
- Avoiding the issue fails the team; punishing first fails the person.
- Fairness and consistency matter more than being liked.
- Know when to involve HR.

**4. In practice — How to prepare**
- Prepare stories: a conflict, a performance issue, a time you supported someone.
- Practise the situation, actions, result structure.
- Say what you learned.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-iv-01-q1).** Asked how you'd handle two senior engineers in open conflict about a design, what's a strong answer?
- ✅ Meet each to understand their views, then bring them together on the shared goal and decision criteria, and make sure a decision gets made and supported — *Understanding, shared criteria and a clear decision resolve it fairly.*
- Pick the more senior one — *Authority isn't reasoning.*
- Ignore it; seniors sort it out — *Conflict festers.*
- Move one to another team immediately — *Drastic without understanding.*

## mg-iv-02 · Execution scenarios
*Skills:* management interview, execution · *Check yourself:* mg-iv-02-q1

**1. What it is — 'The project is slipping; what do you do?'**
- Execution questions test how you deliver: slipping projects, unclear priorities, dependencies on other teams.
- They want concrete steps and trade-offs.

**2. Core ideas — Diagnose before fixing**
- Find the cause: scope growth, unclear requirements, technical debt, staffing, dependencies.
- Options: cut scope, move dates, add focus (not just people), remove blockers.
- Communicate the trade-off to stakeholders.
- Learn from it for the next plan.

**3. The trade-offs — Adding people isn't free**
- Adding engineers late often slows a project down at first.
- Overtime fixes a week, not a quarter.
- Hiding a slip makes it worse.

**4. In practice — How to prepare**
- Have a real story of a project you rescued or a slip you managed.
- Know a few delivery metrics.
- Show how you communicated.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-iv-02-q1).** A project is a month behind with six weeks left. The director suggests adding four engineers. What do you say?
- ✅ New people need onboarding and slow the team at first; look at cutting scope and removing blockers first, and add people only where work can split cleanly — *Late additions often slow delivery; scope and focus are faster levers.*
- Yes, more people always helps — *Not this late.*
- Ask for ten engineers — *Even worse.*
- Promise to catch up with overtime — *Not sustainable for six weeks.*

## mg-iv-03 · Strategy discussions
*Skills:* management interview, strategy · *Check yourself:* mg-iv-03-q1

**1. What it is — Thinking beyond the next sprint**
- Senior manager interviews include strategy: how you'd set direction, build a roadmap, or decide build versus buy.
- They want structured thinking tied to business goals.

**2. Core ideas — Goals, options, choices**
- Start from the business goal and the current situation.
- Lay out options with costs, risks and benefits.
- Make a recommendation and say what you'd measure.
- Explain how you'd bring the team and stakeholders along.

**3. The trade-offs — Clarity over completeness**
- Listing every option without choosing doesn't show judgement.
- Ignoring people and change management makes strategy fail.
- Admit uncertainty and how you'd reduce it.

**4. In practice — How to prepare**
- Prepare a story of a strategic decision you drove.
- Practise build-versus-buy reasoning.
- Know the company's business well enough to connect to it.

**5. Check yourself — One question**
- Answer it, then read the why.

**The question (mg-iv-03-q1).** Asked whether to build an internal tool or buy one, what's the strongest approach?
- ✅ Compare total cost, time to value, how core it is to the business, and risks; recommend one and say what would change your mind — *Structured reasoning tied to the business shows judgement.*
- Always build; engineers like building — *Bias, not analysis.*
- Always buy; it's faster — *Also bias.*
- Ask the team to vote — *Avoids the decision.*
